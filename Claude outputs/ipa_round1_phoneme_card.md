# IPA Phoneme Card Data — Round 1 (English / German / French / Russian / Ukrainian)

This is the data for the **Round 1 card type**: front = one IPA symbol, back = a
basic example word from each language you already know, highlighting where
that same phone occurs. The goal of Round 1 is to anchor every IPA symbol to
a sound you can already produce and recognize, before Round 2/3 introduce
Czech and Slovak spelling for these (and a few new) sounds.

A blank cell means that language does not have this phone as a distinct
sound. Where a language's normal script is Cyrillic, the example word is
given in Cyrillic with the target sound bolded via its letter.

## Methodology & scope

- **Language set for this card is fixed at five**: English, German, French,
  Russian, Ukrainian. Czech and Slovak are deliberately *not* columns here —
  in the 3-round plan they're what Round 2/3 teach (letter → IPA), not
  languages you're anchoring sounds against yet.
- **Length is phonemic** in German (and contrastive in vowel quality too,
  not just duration) — short/long pairs get separate rows.
- **Russian's hard/soft (palatalized) consonant pairs** are far more
  extensive and robust than Ukrainian's: Russian has established minimal
  pairs for pʲ, bʲ, kʲ, ɡʲ, fʲ, vʲ, xʲ, and mʲ (all included below), where
  Ukrainian's corresponding palatalized labials are contested and were
  omitted from the Ukrainian corpus notes. Russian's ц, by contrast, is
  always hard — Russian has no palatalized цʲ, unlike Ukrainian.
- **Russian ч and щ are phonetically distinct from Ukrainian's ч/щ** —
  Russian's is a genuinely more palatal affricate/fricative ([tɕ], [ɕː])
  than Ukrainian's postalveolar [tʃ]. Both get their own rows so the
  difference is explicit rather than papered over, even where the same
  word (чай, "tea") illustrates both.
- **Russian vowel reduction** (akanye/ikanye — unstressed o/a merging
  toward [a], unstressed e/i merging toward [ɪ]) is allophonic, not
  phonemic, so it isn't given separate rows; only the five-to-six stressed
  vowel phonemes are tabled (the ы/[ɨ] row is included since it's a live
  disagreement in the literature, not because it's settled).
- **French nasal vowels** are a phonemic category none of the other four
  languages have, so they get their own short table.
- **French and Russian have no phonemic diphthongs** — sequences that look
  diphthong-like (Fr. "ai", Ru. "ой") are analyzed as vowel+glide, so both
  columns are blank in the diphthong table; that's a real fact, not a gap.
- Cross-language cognates are used as example words wherever one exists
  (e.g. "no", "bread", "house", "hand", "school") so the same row visibly
  shows the same or a related word across languages — helpful for
  recognition, not just coincidence.

## Nasal vowels (French only)

French phonemically nasalizes four vowels — none of the other four
languages have a phonemic nasal-vowel category (English/German/Russian/
Ukrainian nasals are consonants only, never a property of the vowel itself).

| IPA | French |
|---|---|
| ɑ̃ | s**an**s (without) |
| ɔ̃ | b**on** (good) |
| ɛ̃ | p**ain** (bread) |
| œ̃ | **un** (a/one) |
